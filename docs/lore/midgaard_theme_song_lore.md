# The Theme of Midgaard: "The Watched Square"

*Canonical lore document — Midgaard musical identity*

---

## Overview

No composer has been officially credited with Midgaard's theme. The Guard Command's records describe it simply as "the civic march," the Temple of the Resounding Heart calls it "the processional of counted steps," and the Lantern Registry's Night Route index lists it under "standard watch-opening signal." All three institutions believe, with characteristic Midgaard conviction, that they own it. None of them composed it. It emerged the way the city itself emerged — from convergent necessity, shaped by many hands, owned by none.

The piece is not performed so much as *accumulated*. A traveler approaching the North Gate along the Lantern Road first hears it as a distant brass statement carried on wind. By the time they pass through the gate they have absorbed three additional layers they did not consciously notice arriving. By the time they reach the watched square at the city's center they are inside it.

---

## Instrumentation

### The Foundation: Low Brass

Four deep horns — not bright herald trumpets but instruments of civic weight, the kind used to announce curfew and read warrants — carry the melody's spine. They are tuned a half-step flat against common pitch, a deliberate choice made during the Lantern Reforms when the Tarrenwick family's instrument-wright determined that a slightly flattened brass resonance carried farther down paved corridor streets than standard tuning. The flatness became doctrine. It gives the piece its characteristic quality of sounding *almost right*, of being something you recognize before you have consciously identified it.

The low brass does not move quickly. When it moves, it moves with the deliberation of a magistrate's stamp: purposeful, final, load-bearing.

### The Structural Drums

Two kettledrums and a side drum, playing in the march-pattern of the Seven Watches. The pattern is uneven by design — not the simple 4/4 march of military tradition but a seven-beat cycle that reflects Midgaard's civic clock: three heavy beats for the productive watches (Hammer, Ledger, Bell), two lighter beats for the transitional watches (Kindling, Heat), and two very soft beats at the end representing Lantern Watch and Ash Watch, the city folding itself inward toward curfew.

This creates a rhythm that experienced Midgaard citizens recognize instantly and outsiders find slightly wrong, like a sentence with one syllable too many. Road wardens who have walked Midgaard's patrol routes for years find that their footfalls naturally fall into the seven-beat cycle when they return to the city after postings elsewhere. The rhythm has entered the body.

The side drum's snare is kept loose — a deliberate choice to produce a sound closer to the rustle of parchment and the scrape of a quill across ledger-paper than to a military snap.

### The String Section

A chamber string ensemble provides the human texture beneath the civic brass. The strings carry the countermelody — the one that sounds like the people rather than the institution. Where the brass states the oath, the strings enact what happens when people try to live inside it.

The string writing is deliberately crowded: players frequently cross each other's phrases, creating a layered, market-square ambiguity. On careful listening you can isolate individual melodic lines, each one clean and purposeful, but together they create the organized complexity of a well-functioning corridor — directional, purposeful, but full of a dozen simultaneous intentions.

The strings are given the theme's most human moments: a brief, rising phrase that suggests departure (associated with the North Gate); a slower, more reluctant phrase that suggests the weight of staying; a third phrase, heard only in the middle section, that has no directional quality at all, that simply exists in place, which scholars of the Temple have interpreted as the condition of those who could not leave and were not counted.

### The Temple Bells

The bell complement is exact and canonical: two medium bells and one large bell, matching the Temple of the Resounding Heart's own Bell Watch configuration. The large bell sounds the piece's primary time-pulse. The two medium bells carry the memorial signal — two rings after midnight, which tradition interprets as remembrance rather than alarm.

In the theme, the two medium bells are never struck simultaneously. They answer each other, one slightly after the other, with just enough gap between them to make the listener uncertain whether they heard one bell or two. This mirrors the Violet Silence tradition's acknowledgment of names removed from official rolls: present but not counted, sounded but not confirmed.

The bells do not appear throughout the piece. They mark threshold moments — the beginning, the middle transition, and the close — which is architecturally appropriate for a city that frames all its most important functions as threshold governance.

### The Low Choir

Six voices, no more, singing without words. This is the oldest element of the theme, pre-dating the brass arrangement that was formalized during the Lantern Reforms. Temple oral tradition holds that the six-voice chord was sung at the original founding negotiation between the River Factors, the Upland Syndicate, and the Temple Compact — two voices per founding group, a harmony requiring all three to complete.

The choir sings in the modal scale called the Watcher's Mode by Temple musicians, a scale with no direct equivalent in common music theory, built on an interval structure that the Temple's founding notation describes as "the sound of an open eye." It shares characteristics with the Dorian mode — the characteristic natural sixth that gives it a quality of poised uncertainty, neither fully minor in its sorrow nor fully major in its resolution — but the Watcher's Mode flattens one additional degree, creating a passage-note that functions like a threshold: a step that leads somewhere but never quite arrives.

The choir is not always audible. Like a legal doctrine that governs even when not cited, it is present beneath the surface.

### The Volcanic Rumble

Below all of this: a sustained, almost subsonic tremor produced by a string bass and a large drum struck with a padded mallet and immediately dampened. It is felt more than heard. It appears in three places in the piece's structure — at the foundation, at the darkest point of the middle section, and at the very end, after the theme has ostensibly resolved.

This is the Cinderteeth. The Temple's seismological watch maintains records of exactly this sound — a distant sustained resonance in the deep stone that predates specific eruption events by years. Every Midgaard child who has grown up near the Temple has heard the low hum of seismological instruments and associates it with the city's weight: stone on stone, centuries on centuries, debt on debt.

The volcanic rumble never resolves. The theme ends. The rumble continues.

## Structure and Movement

### Opening: The Kindling Watch

The theme begins before dawn, in the space between Ash Watch's curfew and the first lamp-extinguishing of Kindling Watch. The low brass plays alone — a single held note, the root of the Watcher's Mode, flat against common pitch. Not a fanfare. Not an announcement. A fact.

The drums enter on the seven-beat pattern, soft, like footsteps on flagstone. The lamplighters are beginning their rounds. The city is not yet open but it is already in motion.

The string entrance follows: the departure phrase, the one associated with the North Gate, played quietly and in the low register, as though someone is leaving before anyone else is awake. This is not, the Temple notes, the phrase of a citizen departing. This is the phrase of someone who was never fully counted. It appears here, at the opening, before the civic machinery has fully engaged, and does not return until the theme's close.

The choir enters on the sixth beat of the second cycle — unexpected, slightly early, as though the Temple Compact arrived before the River Factors and Upland Syndicate had finished negotiating. Six voices, the Watcher's Mode, wordless, establishing the harmonic space in which all subsequent civic argument will occur.

### The Civic Body: Ledger, Hammer, Heat, Bell Watches

The middle section is the city at full function. All instruments now engaged. The brass carries the main theme — the civic oath melody, which is built on three ascending intervals corresponding to the Three Civic Oaths: Light, Stone, Bread. Each interval reaches upward with institutional certainty. Together they form a melodic shape that any Midgaard herald can sing from memory because they have been required to.

Beneath the brass the strings run their market-square counterpoint. The drums lock into the seven-beat pattern with increasing authority. The bells sound the large one at the Ledger/Hammer transition, establishing the city's loudest hour.

This section has a quality of controlled intensity — everything moving, everything purposeful, nothing wasted. The Guild Concordat would approve of it. The Continuity faction would say this is what the city sounds like when functioning correctly. The Reckoning faction would note that the Ledger of Debts was processed during exactly this kind of efficient civic function.

Buried in the strings, audible only on careful listening, the phrase of those who could not leave and were not counted plays through once and is absorbed back into the counterpoint. It does not interrupt. It does not insist. It is simply there, as those persons were simply there.

### The Threshold: Violet Compact Recognition

At the precise midpoint of the piece — the pivot on which it turns — the instrumentation strips back to brass alone, playing a four-beat phrase not in the Watcher's Mode but in a secondary modal color that the Lantern Registry's old notation calls the Violet Interval. It sounds like a door being opened to a room that is cooler than expected.

The Violet Interval phrase is short. Eight beats. It states a threshold and crosses it.

The low choir returns here, its only appearance in the middle section. On this appearance, one of the six voices sustains a note outside the main harmony — technically a dissonance, justified in Temple musical doctrine as "the voice of the unsealed name." It resolves on the last beat of the Violet Interval phrase, absorbed back into the chord, which is itself the appropriate institutional fate of things handled through the registry system.

The volcanic rumble sounds here for the second time, slightly louder than at the opening. It continues into the next section.

### The Lantern Watch and the Deepening

The theme's longest and most architecturally complex section. The pace does not slow, but the quality changes — from the productive chaos of Hammer Watch to the watchful, tightening attention of Lantern Watch. The string density increases. The drums' seven-beat pattern becomes more deliberate, each soft beat at the end of the cycle given slightly more weight than before.

The brass introduces a second theme here, heard for the first time: a descending phrase where the civic oath melody was ascending. The intervals are the same — Light, Stone, Bread — but inverted. Descending. Where the oaths reach upward in hope of being kept, the descending phrase asks what happens when they are not.

This second theme is not resolution. It is the Unindexed Years given musical form: the same promise, played backward, covering the same distance in the wrong direction. The Reckoning faction's musicians have historically given this phrase the unofficial title "the Ledger of Debts Theme," though the Lantern Registry prefers to call it "the Ash-and-Iron transition" and decline to specify what it represents.

The strings' crowded counterpoint here takes on its most complex texture, multiple melodic lines stacking against each other in a way that should produce cacophony but instead produces a controlled density — order maintained under pressure, which is, structurally, what the city did during the Ashfall Monsoon, the Evermeet Collapse, and the northern supply disruptions simultaneously. The city did not break. It also did not pause to count who had been lost.

The choir's third and final appearance occurs in this section, singing the founding six-voice chord in full voice for the first time. All six voices, all in harmony, no dissonance. The oaths, fully stated. Midgaard's civic self-conception at its most complete.

Immediately after the choir's full statement, the descending brass phrase plays again. Alone. Without accompaniment.

### The Ash Watch and the Close

The piece does not end dramatically. Midgaard does not permit dramatic endings — endings have to be documented, filed, and assigned a reference number.

The instruments withdraw in reverse order of arrival: strings first, then choir, then drums reducing to the single large bell marking the Ash Watch's beginning. The brass holds its root note — the same flat-tuned foundational note with which the piece opened.

The two medium bells sound here for the last time. One. Then the other, slightly after. Memorial, not alarm.

The departure phrase from the opening returns in the strings, this time in the upper register, played with the quality of something seen at a distance rather than something felt underfoot. It is the same phrase — someone leaving before they are counted, before the ledger opens, before the Watch begins — but at the close it carries both the weight of those who departed long ago and the specific implication that this is happening right now, during the Ash Watch, in the service corridors where shadow residents have their undocumented existence, in the subterranean threshold spaces below the Violet Reliquary Arch where civic jurisdiction acknowledges limits.

The brass resolves — incompletely. The final chord is the Watcher's Mode's characteristic suspended quality: not the resolution of a piece ending but the resolution of a watch ending, knowing the next watch begins before this one has fully closed.

The volcanic rumble continues for two additional beats after all other instruments have stopped.

Then silence.

## Performance Contexts

**The Night of First Lamps** — The theme is performed in sections, each section marking a different checkpoint on the ceremonial re-lighting of the Lantern Reform routes. The opening is played at the first lamp. The civic body section plays at the Granite Arcade. The Violet Compact section plays at the Reliquary Arch. The Lantern Watch section plays at the Watch Commander's station. The close plays at the Temple of the Resounding Heart.

**Civic Grief Watch** — On this day the theme is performed at half-speed, without the drums. Only brass, strings, and choir. The volcanic rumble is held twice as long. The two medium bells are struck seven times — once for each Watch — rather than twice.

**Gate Opening Ceremony** — The first four beats of the brass statement are played by the Gate Warden's post horn at each morning gate-opening. Most citizens do not consciously recognize it as the theme's opening, but those who have been away for long journeys report that hearing it at the gate feels like the city putting its hand on their shoulder.

**Warrant Reading** — The Bell Watch's official notice period is preceded by the brass playing only the descending phrase — the Ash-and-Iron transition — once, unaccompanied. This has been practice since the Ash-and-Iron Decades. Originally it was a time signal. Over time it has acquired the additional quality of institutional acknowledgment: what follows is civic function; it carries the weight of all civic function that preceded it, including the parts not discussed in open session.

## The Dispute About the Theme's Name

The piece has three official names:

- The Guard Command calls it "The March of the Watched Square."
- The Temple of the Resounding Heart calls it "The Processional of the Uncounted Steps."
- The Lantern Registry calls it "Standard Watch-Opening Signal, Civic Grade."

The Reckoning faction has proposed a fourth name: "The Ledger Made Sound." The Continuity faction has rejected the proposal on procedural grounds and referred it to the Charter Sovereignty Office for review. The review has been pending for eleven years.

The Sealed Families, when asked, refer to it as "the city's music," which is technically accurate and commits to nothing.

No one calls it by the name that children in the day-mark laborers' districts use when they hum it to themselves while waiting in the intake queues: "The Counting Song." But that is what it is. It counts. It was designed to count. Whether it counts everyone is the question on which Midgaard divides.
